## The Bully and the Broadcast: Why America’s Media Must Stand Together Now

Jen Psaki’s recent commentary — warning that Trump behaves like a bully who keeps taking your lunch money if you appease him — is more than a sharp political line. It’s a perfect metaphor for what happens when a president uses regulatory power to punish coverage he doesn’t like. Bullies escalate. Bullies test boundaries. And bullies don’t stop until someone stands up and says: No more.

That moment is here for America’s media.

The FCC’s posture toward ABC isn’t just a dispute with one network. It’s a warning shot aimed at the entire broadcast ecosystem — television, radio, and print alike. When a president signals that he’s willing to use regulatory tools as weapons, every newsroom becomes vulnerable, regardless of size, ideology, or audience.

And I say that as someone who has lived inside the quirks of American radio. When my 2009 HHR was totaled, I replaced it with a used 2011 model whose AM was sporadic, FM didn’t work at all, but XM worked flawlessly. On long drives — especially our November 2016 trip to Florida — XM was the constant companion. No hunting for stations. No fading signals. No commercials. Just the same channel, coast‑to‑coast, for an entire week. It was a reminder that each layer of broadcast media has its own infrastructure, its own vulnerabilities, and its own role in the American information landscape. The “3M’s” — AM, FM, XM — are different worlds, but all three depend on regulatory stability and freedom from political retaliation.

That’s why this moment matters.

The First Amendment isn’t a courtesy extended by government. It’s a constraint placed upon it — a constitutional boundary that prevents officials from punishing dissent, criticism, or unfavorable coverage. When a president threatens licenses, pressures regulators, or uses administrative tools to intimidate broadcasters, he crosses that boundary. And once that line is crossed, loyalty offers no protection. Even outlets often perceived as friendly — Fox News, OAN, Newsmax — should not assume immunity. History shows that when retaliation becomes normalized, it eventually reaches everyone.

As America approaches its 250th year, the stakes could not be clearer. Benjamin Franklin’s warning during the Revolution still applies: “We must all hang together, or we shall all hang separately.” That wasn’t just a call for unity — it was a recognition that freedom survives only when those who depend on it defend it together.

Television networks, radio stations, newspapers, magazines, digital publishers — they must respond to this moment with unified resolve. A single outlet cannot withstand government intimidation alone. But a united press can.

Defending the First Amendment is not optional. It is the responsibility of every newsroom that relies on it, every broadcaster who transmits it, and every journalist who embodies it.

And it’s the responsibility of all of us — listeners, viewers, readers — to insist that the press remain free, independent, and unafraid.
