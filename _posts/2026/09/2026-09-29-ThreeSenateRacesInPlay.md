## [These 3 Senate races weren’t supposed to be close. That’s a warning sign for the GOP.](https://www.ms.now/opinion/senate-kansas-mississippi-south-carolina-project-47)

_[Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/), [South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) and [Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/) aren’t supposed to be battlegrounds. So why are [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) sweating them_

By Kai Ma

Sept. 29, 2026, 12:45 PM EDT

Months before Election Day, [Democratic](https://www.democrats.org/) and [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) strategists pore over the [Senate](https://www.senate.gov/) map like a game of Risk, deciding where to wage the fights that could determine control.

The top tier consists of swing states, where a closely divided electorate could be nudged either way by favorable conditions, a strong candidate or a timely infusion of cash.

The second tier consists of states where one side might have a shot if everything goes its way.

Then there’s the third tier: states that barely warrant a conversation. These are places no one expects to be close, where the opposing party hasn’t won statewide in years and there’s a thin bench of plausible candidates.

In a normal year, [Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/), [South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) and [Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/) would barely register on the [Democratic](https://www.democrats.org/) [Senate](https://www.senate.gov/) map. But recent polls show [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) running surprisingly close in all three. Even [Nate Silver](https://www.natesilver.net/) recently called the results “[a little weird.](https://www.natesilver.net/p/the-blue-wave-is-mostly-in-red-states)”

The odds are still stacked against [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) sweeping through the Deep South and Great Plains. And, as everyone says this time of year, no poll matters as much as what happens at the ballot box. But [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) are now forced to spend time and money defending states that should have been easy wins.

Here’s a closer look at why [Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/), [Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/) and [South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) have become unexpectedly competitive.

[Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/)
[Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/) is the clearest case. The state hasn’t elected a [Democrat](https://www.democrats.org/)ic senator since 1932, and [President](https://www.whitehouse.gov/) [Donald Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) carried it by 16 points just two years ago. But [Democrat](https://www.democrats.org/) [Adam Hamilton](https://hamiltonforkansas.com/), a United Methodist pastor and first-time candidate, has turned what should have been a sleepy re-election for [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) [Sen.](https://www.senate.gov/) [Roger Marshall](https://www.marshall.senate.gov/) into a real contest.

[Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/) also has a history of bucking its deep-red reputation. It regularly elects [Democratic](https://www.democrats.org/) governors, and in 2022, voters rejected an effort to strip abortion rights from the state constitution by nearly 20 points.

That doesn’t make [Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/) a swing state. But it does reveal a sizable bloc of moderate and independent-minded voters willing to break with the [GOP](https://www.gop.com/) — one reason this race may be closer than the state’s red reputation suggests.

[Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/)

[Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/) is ruby red, but its unusually large Black electorate gives [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) a substantial base. [Black voters make up a larger share of eligible voters there than in any other state](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/hawaii-has-the-largest-share-of-asian-eligible-voters/), helping keep some statewide races closer than the state’s partisan reputation might suggest.

[Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) also have an unusually well-suited candidate in [Scott Colom](https://scottcolom.com/), a seventh-generation [Mississippian](https://www.ms.gov/) and district attorney running as a pro-law-enforcement [Democrat](https://www.democrats.org/). [Colom outraised Republican Sen. Cindy Hyde-Smith](https://cdispatch.com/news/colom-raises-another-765k-outpacing-hyde-smith/) in the most recently reported quarter and has drawn the endorsement of [Pete Buttigieg](https://www.ms.now/news/pete-buttigieg-scott-colom-mississippi-senate), who is urging [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) to expand their map to include [Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/).

[South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/)
[South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) has not elected a [Democratic](https://www.democrats.org/) senator since 1998. But [Democrat](https://www.democrats.org/) [Annie Andrews](https://www.drannieandrews.com/) is running neck and neck with [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) [Sen.](https://www.senate.gov/) [Darline Graham](https://www.dgraham.senate.gov/), the sister of the late [Sen.](https://www.senate.gov/) [Lindsey Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lindsey_Graham?wprov=sfla1).

After her brother’s death, [Gov.](https://governor.sc.gov/) [Henry McMaster](https://governor.sc.gov/governors-biography) appointed [Graham](https://www.dgraham.senate.gov/) to fill his seat, and [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) backed her through a contentious [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) primary. But polls show the race as essentially tied, turning what should have been a [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) coronation into the kind of statewide general election contest [South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) hasn’t seen in 16 years.

[South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) may prove to be the most candidate-specific of the three races: a newly appointed senator, a contentious primary and a still-unsettled electorate may be the key factors. [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) can explain away the other races, too. [Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/) has a moderate streak, while [Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/) has an unusually large Black electorate.

But when three supposedly safe [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) states all look competitive at once, the simplest explanation may be that [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) are struggling more broadly.

Of course, history demands skepticism of eye-popping [Democratic](https://www.democrats.org/) numbers in red states. Polls badly underestimated [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) support in 2016 and even more dramatically in 2020. But they performed considerably better in the 2018 and 2022 midterms. They may be underestimating [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) turnout now, or have some other oversight.

What is notable now is that it is already late September, and the polling has not tightened. [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/)’ advantage on the generic ballot has grown since the beginning of the month, while competitive margins in deep-red [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) territory have persisted.

None of this means [Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/), [South Carolina](https://www.sc.gov/) or [Mississippi](https://www.ms.gov/) is turning purple, much less blue. [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) may not win any of these seats. But when [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) are forced to fight in places they normally would not have to defend, that may be one of the clearest clues about the races in the rest of the country, hiding in plain sight.
