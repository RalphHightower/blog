## [How Trump decimated America’s cybersecurity](https://www.ms.now/opinion/trump-cybersecurity-cuts-cyberattack-tech)

_Tech outages sparked fears of a cyberattack — which the U.S. is much more vulnerable to thanks to the [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) [administration](https://www.whitehouse.gov/administration/)’s rampant cuts._

By [Michael A. Cohen](https://www.ms.now/author/michael-a-cohen-2)

Nov. 22, 2026, 6:00 AM EST

It started with [Microsoft](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/18/microsoft-put-the-ai-threat-to-software-behind-it-then-came-the-outage.html) last Thursday. Then it was the airlines on Friday, and a [nationwide ground stop](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/14/politics/faa-ground-stop-what-we-know). Next was [Workday](https://people.com/workday-suffers-outage-one-day-after-microsoft-faced-similar-disruption-12254513), [Chase Bank, United Airlines, Apple](https://www.vanityfair.com/story/cybersecurity-outage?srsltid=AYCX9vILg-DOVp7OYumIIaPVEio6l7fylMpmx6DRlk_X02MVD4Qf5QaH), and perhaps most ominously, the [National Security Agency](https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/17/politics/highly-classified-national-security-communications-network-goes-down), as technology and internet outages swept the country. What began as a rough week for the nation’s contingent of IT guys quickly became fodder for conspiracy theorists, who were convinced the U.S. was experiencing a coordinated cyberattack.

From nearly all accounts, the coincidence of technological issues is the result of an unprecedented number of technological upgrades, due in large part to the rapid adoption of AI tools. The U.S. is not under attack from keyboard-clicking actors working for foreign governments or shadowy terrorist groups.

But what if we were?

How vulnerable is America to a major cyberattack?

The answer is more than we should be – and thanks, in large part, to the [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) [administration](https://www.whitehouse.gov/administration/).

Last year, the [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) [administration](https://www.whitehouse.gov/administration/) [took a hatchet](https://www.nytimes.com/2025/10/22/us/politics/trump-cyber-threats.html) to the [Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA)](https://www.cisa.gov/), cutting millions from its budget and pushing out a third of its staff, or more than a thousand employees. (Hundreds of other staffers [were reassigned](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/10/homeland-security-reassigns-hundreds-of-cisa-cyber-staffers-to-support-trumps-deportation-crackdown/) within the [Department of Homeland Security](https://www.dhs.gov) to aid in the p[president](https://www.whitehouse.gov/)’s mass deportation campaign). [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/)’s 2027 budget [proposed cutting](https://www.propublica.org/article/cisa-election-security-donald-trump?com) an additional \$700 million in funding, which could lead to 900 more layoffs.

[CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/) has been without a permanent director since [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) fired the previous director, [Chris Krebs](https://www.linkedin.com/in/christopherckrebs/), after he [publicly affirmed](https://gizmodo.com/whos-going-to-protect-americas-infrastructure-after-trump-gutted-cisa-2000812905) that the [2020 election](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/federalelections2020.pdf) had not been stolen. ([Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/)’s nominee for the position, Michael LoSapio, [said in a confirmation hearing](https://www.huffpost.com/entry/trump-nominees-still-cant-say-what-happened-jan-6-2021_n_6aff184de4b0f8ebfa4a85ac?wa) on Tuesday that he wouldn’t characterize the [January 6, 2021](https://youtube.com/playlist?list=PL0W4zBOS45K_NAfZ-MFphiK1y73LVa05q&si=f1A62IKxQ7ZjjbDK) assault on the Capitol as an “attack,” which doesn’t exactly instill confidence about his potential leadership at the agency.)

[CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/)’s role in contradicting [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/)’s false claims about the 2020 election [almost certainly played an outsized role](https://www.propublica.org/article/cisa-election-security-donald-trump?) in the [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/)’s determination to so aggressively hamstring the agency.

> With AI tools, the steady drumbeat of cyber incidents – both large and small – is only likely to increase in the years to come. And [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) has left America with little defense for what is coming.

The result is that the U.S. – both in the public and private sector – is simply less equipped to deal with potential cyberattacks. [Allison Pytlak](https://www.stimson.org/ppl/allison-pytlak/), Senior Fellow and Director of the [Cyber Program](https://www.stimson.org/program/cyber/) at the [Stimson Center](https://www.stimson.org/), told me, “these cuts put Americans at greater risk of individual cyber threats” because they have “reduced the pool of qualified individuals who work on cyber defense. If you take out a third of any workforce, it’s going to create problems.”

That means the U.S. is more vulnerable to potential cyberstrikes.

Already this summer, a cyberattack [linked to Iran](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/07/30/us/politics/minnesota-water-cyberattack-iran.html) targeted U.S. water systems. Another incident in [Europe](https://european-union.europa.eu/index_en) [shut down grocery store](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/europe-freezer-hack-cybercrime-are-smart-devices-vulnerable/), sowing panic. However, digital incursions from other nations generally leave a digital trail. If [China](https://www.gov.cn/) or [North Korea](http://www.naenara.com.kp/) circumvent U.S. cyber defenses and wreak havoc, the U.S. can retaliate. So there are limits to how far any country would go, for fear that a massive cyberattack could provoke a U.S. response.

But with the wider adoption of AI technology, the hacker playing field, says [Pytlak](https://www.stimson.org/ppl/allison-pytlak/), is being leveled – and the barrier to entry is far lower. That means the ability of non-state actors or criminals waging cyberattacks on private-sector systems is likely to increase.

That’s a crucial part of what [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/) is supposed to do – help the private sector construct impenetrable cyber defenses – and which [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/)’s budget cuts have made more difficult.

In September, the [agency announced](https://www.cybersecuritydive.com/news/cisa-cybersecurity-assessments-ending/829371/) it was significantly reducing the reviews and assessments it had previously performed for critical infrastructure networks. Such moves could leave hospitals, banks, energy providers, and transportation systems more vulnerable to attack. But the cutbacks – and diminished cooperation between [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/) and the private sector – also make it harder for companies to defend themselves, which in turn puts Americans at risk of cyber incursions.  For under-resourced state and local governments, the challenge is even greater.

To be sure, none of this means we’re likely on the cusp of a cyber-catastrophe like the one famously depicted in 2007’s “[Live Free or Die Hard](https://www.nytimes.com/2007/07/03/movies/03jame.html).” But with AI tools, the steady drumbeat of cyber incidents – both large and small – is only likely to increase in the years to come. And [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) has left America with little defense for what is coming.

When [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) take over [Congress](https://www.congress.gov/) in January, they will certainly have their hands full, but ensuring that [CISA](https://www.cisa.gov/) is fully funded and has a mandate and the tools to work with the private sector to increase the country’s cyber defenses should be at the top of the list (thankfully, it’s a priority that many congressional [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) also support). The headlines from the past week portend a potentially dark cyber future, but it’s also one we can avoid with better leadership in [Washington](https://dc.gov/).
