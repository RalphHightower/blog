## [Did Democrats win, or did Donald Trump just lose?](https://www.ms.now/news/midterms-election-democrats-trump)

_Fresh off a midterm wave, party bigwigs gathered in [Denver](https://www.denvergov.org/Home) to debate whether voters embraced them or just rejected [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/). Their answer could shape 2028._

By [Hunter Woodall](https://www.ms.now/author/hunter-woodall)

Nov. 14, 2026, 7:00 AM EST

[DENVER](https://www.denvergov.org/Home) — Happiness risks being a fleeting feeling for [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) in [Donald Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/)’s America.

After years of struggling to find a way to defeat and counter the past and present president, the wide-blue wave of last week’s midterms has given [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) a sense of elation that has long eluded them.

As party faithful gathered in [Colorado](https://www.colorado.gov/) for a post-midterm “What’s Next” event, the question quickly became what voters were really saying, and whether the results were a sign of [Democratic](https://www.democrats.org/) strength, or primarily of the struggles of [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) and his allies.

“Not only did we win, we cleaned their clock, and so as a consequence, people think that we’re not weak, woke and losers anymore,” said Mitch Landrieu, the former mayor of [New Orleans](https://nola.gov/home/), [who is eying a Democratic run for the White House](https://www.ms.now/news/mitch-landrieu-the-former-mayor-of-new-orleans-is-considering-a-run-for-president). “Now, it doesn’t mean that they believe in us and they trust us. They just believe ‘I’m going to give you another chance’ and now we have to demonstrate that we deserve what it is that they gave us.”

Recent history gives [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) reason for caution. [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/)’s first victory in [2016](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/federalelections2020.pdf) gave [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) unified control of [Washington](https://dc.gov/). The midterms two years later easily handed [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) the [House](https://www.house.gov/), but [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) added to their [Senate](https://www.senate.gov/) majority. And when [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) won back the [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/) and full control of [Washington](https://dc.gov/) in 2020, the pandemic and the fallout from the [Jan. 6, 2021, attack on the U.S. Capitol](https://youtube.com/playlist?list=PL0W4zBOS45K_NAfZ-MFphiK1y73LVa05q&si=SxfqEnEY0k25mFOP) shadowed the news of victory.

Now the party is at a turning point it hasn’t seen in decades. It has a chance to change how people see a party that was toxic in parts of the country just two years ago. Cost-of-living woes and the [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) [administration](https://www.whitehouse.gov/administration/)’s entanglement in the war with Iran helped make that possible.

The party’s old baggage hasn’t gone away, though. Some of it dates to the [Biden era](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/). Some goes back to the state-legislative ground [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) lost in the [2010 Tea Party wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea_Party_movement?wprov=sfla1), during [Barack Obama](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/)’s first midterm as president. Still, wins up and down the ballot have given [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) an opening to rebuild relationships with voters that had grown brittle and fractured.

“[Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) started changing what they were speaking about,” said [Sandra Jauregui](https://sandrafornevada.com/), who won her race last week to become [Nevada](https://www.nv.gov/)’s next [lieutenant governor](https://www.ltgov.nv.gov/). In 2024, [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) became the first [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) since the [George W. Bush](https://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/) era to carry the state in a presidential race. “We finally started listening to voters instead of telling them what they should care about.”

Even before the midterms, some [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) worried that big wins would hide the party’s longer-term problems, which no single election can fix. They also worried that lopsided victories would breed overconfidence. That could hurt the party’s chances of winning back the [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/) in 2028, and with them any hope of pushing the [MAGA](https://www.magapac.com/) movement out of politics and into the history books as they prepare to face someone other than [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) in a presidential campaign.

“[Dems](https://www.democrats.org/) are sure to ignore any lessons and assume that people like everything about them only to be devastated in 2028,” a national [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) operative said.

Some of the party’s most standout performances may not carry over to 2028. Last week’s statewide wins in [Texas](https://www.texas.gov/) have [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) imagining the state going blue in a presidential race, which would fundamentally redraw the path to 270 electoral votes and the presidency. But [Texas](https://www.texas.gov/) is enormously expensive to compete in, as this year’s [Senate](https://www.senate.gov/) race showed. And the next [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) presidential nominee is unlikely to carry the political and public baggage of [Ken Paxton](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/), the state’s impeached and acquitted [GOP](https://www.gop.com/) attorney general who lost the [Senate](https://www.senate.gov/) race this fall.

[Kansas](https://portal.kansas.gov/) is similar. [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) have now won the governor’s office in the last three elections there, and voters are sending a moderate from the party to the U.S. [Senate](https://www.senate.gov/) for the first time in almost 90 years. But in presidential years, Kansas remains comfortably [Republican](https://www.gop.com/).

American politics doesn’t happen in a vacuum, either. [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) are on track to soon be back to their usual fights over messaging, approach and progressive-versus-moderate positioning. But the path forward will also depend on what [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) do.

In 2008, after heavy losses in the 2006 midterms during [George W. Bush](https://georgewbush-whitehouse.archives.gov/)’s second term, [Republicans](https://www.gop.com/) effectively pushed the incumbent aside as the party’s undisputed leader.

The [GOP](https://www.gop.com/) could try the same thing with [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/). But that would mean breaking a hold that once looked fragile. A decade ago, the reality television star and real estate mogul upended the [Republican](https://www.gop.com/) establishment and remade the right in his own image. His grip held even after he lost the [2020 election](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/federalelections2020.pdf) and a mob of his supporters attacked the [U.S. Capitol](http://www.visitthecapitol.gov/) and threw the transfer of power into chaos. Then he won back the [White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/) in [2024](https://www.fec.gov/resources/cms-content/documents/2024presgeresults.pdf) decisively.

[Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) have their own warning signs. Some of this month’s winners got there by keeping their distance from the party.

Both parties face a trust deficit, which is hardly surprising. The irony for [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) is that they are being swept into power with help from dissatisfied former [Trump](https://www.donaldjtrump.com/) voters. They now have a chance to rebuild because of the man they spent so long struggling to beat. The challenge fast approaching is what they can actually deliver to a dissatisfied public.

[Amanda Litman](https://runforsomething.net/about/), who leads the young progressive focused organization [Run for Something](https://runforsomething.net/), called last week’s results “very much a primal scream in the form of a vote.”

“Enough [Democrats](https://www.democrats.org/) were able to talk about the source of that rage in a way that made them a viable outlet for voters to pick,” [Litman](https://www.linkedin.com/in/amandalitman/) said. “But I think it is 95% ‘throw the bums out,’ and 5%, ‘OK, let’s try these bums.’“
