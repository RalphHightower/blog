<details id="ahab-complex-2020">
  <summary><strong>Ahab Complex (2020)</strong> (noun)</summary>
  <p><em>pronunciation:</em> /ˈeɪ.hæb ˈkɒm.plɛks twɛn.ti ˈtwɛn.ti/</p>

1. A persistent, identity‑defining fixation on the 2020 election loss, characterized by continual pursuit of new procedural, rhetorical, or symbolic “sightings” of potential reversal despite the certified outcome.
2. A political analogue to Captain Ahab’s pursuit of the white whale in Herman Melville’s <em>Moby‑Dick</em>, wherein the chase becomes more important than the attainable objective.
3. The narrative pattern in which each audit, lawsuit, recount, seizure, or clerical irregularity is treated as another glimpse of the whale’s spout — a reason to resume or intensify the pursuit.

  <p><em>origin:</em> Coined in Ralphian commentary drawing structural parallels between Melville’s depiction of obsession and Donald Trump’s continued focus on the 2020 election.</p>

  <p><em>notes:</em> Often referenced alongside the quotation from <em>Moby‑Dick</em>, “All my means are sane; my motive and my object mad,” used here to illustrate the contrast between procedural actions and the unattainable goal.</p>

  <p><em>cross‑reference:</em> See also <a href="#dumbassedness">dumbassedness</a>, <a href="#institutional-overreachery">institutional overreachery</a>.</p>

## B

<details markdown='1' id="blameshifter"><summary><strong>blameshifter</strong></summary>

blameshifter
noun

1. A person who redirects fault or responsibility away from themselves and onto others, especially when they are primarily responsible for the problem.

2. Someone who habitually avoids accountability by assigning blame to coworkers, subordinates, external factors, or circumstances.

Usage examples:
• “He missed the deadline but became a blameshifter the moment the manager asked what happened.”
• “Every project failure turned into a hunt for targets—the blameshifter never owned his part.”

Related terms:
• buck-passer
• responsibility dodger
• scapegoater
• deflector
• blame deflector

</details>

<details markdown='1' id="blamestorming"><summary><strong>blamestorming</strong></summary>

blamestorming
noun

1. A meeting or discussion in which the primary purpose is to assign blame for a problem rather than to solve it.

2. A workplace or organizational practice where participants focus on identifying who is at fault instead of addressing root causes or corrective actions.

Usage examples:
• “The outage could have been fixed in an hour, but the team spent the morning blamestorming instead.”
• “Blamestorming sessions were common in that office—fault mattered more than solutions.”

Related terms:
• blame game
• scapegoating
• finger-pointing
• accountability dodging
• responsibility deflection

</details>

<details markdown='1' id="camera-hugger"><summary><strong>camera hugger</strong></summary>

noun

1. A public official who consistently positions themselves near cameras, photographers, or media crews at public events, especially ceremonies, groundbreakings, ribbon cuttings, or press opportunities, regardless of their actual involvement in the project.

2. One who seeks visibility through physical proximity to symbolic props (such as gold shovels, oversized scissors, hard hats, or podiums) in order to appear central to an accomplishment.

3. A subtype of glory hog whose primary method of self-promotion is being photographed rather than performing substantive work.

Usage examples:
• “The bill had bipartisan sponsors, but the camera hugger showed up first when the ribbon was cut.”
• “He wasn’t on the committee, but he’s a camera hugger — so of course he found a gold shovel and a photographer.”

Related terms:
• grandstander
• photo-op opportunist
• spotlight-chaser
• press-release hero.

</details>

<details markdown='1' id="December-32nd"><summary><strong>December 32nd</strong></summary>

December 32nd
noun

1. An impossible date used to force revenue, transactions, or activity into a reporting period after the legitimate calendar has ended.

2. A symbolic term for an organizational culture in which leadership overrides established rules, timelines, or accounting standards to achieve desired numbers or appearances.

3. A shorthand for executive-level manipulation of schedules or data, typically resulting in regulatory scrutiny, internal confusion, or blamestorming.

Usage examples:
• “The quarter was short, so they booked the deal on December 32nd to make the numbers work.”
• “December 32nd became a running joke—proof the leadership thought they could negotiate with the calendar.”

Related terms:
• off-calendar booking
• earnings management
• window dressing
• cooking the books
• leadership override

</details>

<details id="dumbassedness">
  <summary><strong>dumbassedness</strong> (noun)</summary>
  dumbassedness
noun
  <p><em>pronunciation:</em> / ˈdəm‑ˌba‑səd‑nəs /</p>
  dumb·ass·ed·ness \ ˈdəm‑ˌba‑səd‑nəs \

1. the quality or state of engaging in behavior that ignores evidence, experience, or basic common sense, often with great confidence
2. a persistent pattern of decisions marked by preventable error, misplaced certainty, or enthusiastic misunderstanding
3. (informal) the active practice of being a dumbass

---

Synonyms
folly, boneheadedness, cluelessness, misjudgment, tomfoolery, nonsense

Antonyms
prudence, sound judgment, competence, sense

---

> He approached the task with admirable enthusiasm and breathtaking dumbassedness.

> In a moment of pure dumbassedness, she attempted to fix the server by “just rebooting everything.”

<details id="flying-fortress">
  <summary><strong>Flying Fortress</strong> (noun)</summary>
  <p><em>pronunciation:</em> fly·​ing for·​tress | \ ˈflī-iŋ  ˈfȯr-trəs  \</p>
  
Boeing VC-25 is a military version of the Boeing 747 airliner, modified for presidential transport and commonly operated by the United States Air Force (USAF) as Air Force One, the call sign of any U.S. Air Force aircraft carrying the president of the United States.

The presidential air transport fleet consists of two specially configured Boeing 747-200B's — tail numbers 28000 and 29000 — with the Air Force designation VC-25. When the president is aboard either aircraft, or any Air Force aircraft, the radio call sign is "Air Force One."

Principal differences between the VC-25 and the standard Boeing 747, other than the number of passengers carried, are the electronic and communications equipment, self-contained baggage loader, front and aft air-stairs, and the capability for in-flight refueling. Besides the secure avionics, it is also hardened to withstand an electromagnetic pulse generated by a nuclear bomb.

Accommodations for the president include an executive suite consisting of a stateroom (with dressing room, lavatory and shower) and the president's office. A conference/dining room is also available for the president, his family and staff. Other separate accommodations are provided for guests, senior staff, Secret Service and security personnel and the news media.

Two galleys provide up to 100 meals at one sitting. Six passenger lavatories, including disabled access facilities, are provided as well as a rest area and mini-galley for the aircrew. The VC-25 also has a compartment outfitted with medical equipment and supplies for minor medical emergencies.

paycheck diversity
noun

1. The practice of maintaining income from more than one employer at the same time to increase financial stability, reduce risk, or create multiple safety nets.

2. A personal or household strategy in which separate jobs, roles, or contracts provide overlapping sources of income, often used to buffer against layoffs, organizational instability, or economic uncertainty.

3. A humorous or satirical term describing the deliberate spread of earning risk across multiple workplaces, especially when one employer is volatile, political, or poorly managed.

Usage examples:
• “After seeing how unstable the department was, she decided paycheckdiversity was the safest move.”
• “Paycheck diversity kept the household steady even when one employerwent through reorganizations.”

Related terms:
• income buffering
• risk-spread employment
• dual employment
• financial redundancy
